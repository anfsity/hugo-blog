## Unmatched Emotional Flow

I don't remember where I first saw it—probably during one of Fanshi's livestreams, where he remarked that most of his MADs are meant to convey a single, complete arc of emotional flow (I think he was talking about an *Oregairu* MAD). Ever since then, I've paid close attention to whether a work's emotional arc feels whole—and *Liz and the Blue Bird* is nothing short of perfection in this regard.

Speaking of emotional flow, I think the most classic example is Fanshi's *Uma Musume* MAD—[The Fall of the Undefeated, The Resurrection of a Miracle! "T.E.I.O"](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1jF411B7sw/). Even if you've never seen Season 2 of *Uma Musume*, you can still be moved by the sheer emotion overflowing from it (*Uma Musume* Season 2 really is so good 😭).

Well actually, if you strip the movie down to its plot outline, there really isn't much to it—a 90-minute runtime is practically "more than generous" for such a skeletal framework. Yet it never once feels hollow or padded, because the film doesn't drive its narrative through "and then X happened." Instead, it relies on a seamless, uninterrupted current of emotion woven from visuals and music, pulling the viewer along in a steady flow. The climax of the plot is, at the same time, the emotional breaking point for both protagonists.

From the seemingly calm everyday life at the beginning—tinged with a faint, subtle distance—to the middle section where their strides gradually fall out of sync over future plans and ensemble practice, every ounce of emotion quietly settles layer upon layer. Then comes the third movement, the moment when the true "Liz" and the true "Blue Bird" are revealed to the audience through the music—and a whole hour of pent-up tension and misalignment bursts forth all at once in those three short minutes. It is utterly exhilarating.

The emotional flow in *Liz and the Blue Bird* is uniquely its own: it moves from restraint and misalignment $\to$ release and resolution. Naoko Yamada spends a full hour laying out a single aching question for the audience: "When they mean the world to each other, why can't they ever seem to stay in step—not in their footsteps, not in conversation, and not even in their duet? 😭" To use a slightly inappropriate metaphor, Mizore keeps her eyes fixed on Nozomi, and whenever the other band members interrupt their conversation and pull Nozomi away, ~~it almost feels like Mizore is getting NTR'd~~. What makes it even more brilliant, though, is that this restraint isn't a one-woman show on Mizore's part. The first half is anchored in Mizore's perspective, so the audience naturally steps into Liz's shoes alongside her—treading on eggshells, terrified that loosening her grip for even a second will let Nozomi fly away (guided by Yamada's framing, I naturally assumed Mizore was Liz and Nozomi was the Blue Bird). Yet on the other side, Nozomi has been quietly carrying her own unspoken weight all along—whether facing her future after graduation, Mizore's sheer talent, or Ririka growing closer to Mizore. Nozomi never lets any fierce emotion show; instead, beneath her usual breezy cheerfulness, she occasionally lets slip a hesitant pause, a fleeting hint of distance.

![This kouhai is so cute](/post/essays/images/Pasted%20image%2020261121231121.png)

Such an understated misalignment is a delicacy that demands to be savored slowly (I didn't even fully appreciate it while watching, but the more I write this post, the more delicious it feels).

And precisely because the first half keeps this misalignment hidden beneath the surface, the "release" and "resolution" in the second half hit with such lingering force.

First comes the release in the third movement. The moment Mizore finally plays her oboe without holding anything back, the hour-long misalignment of perspective shatters in an instant—Mizore, who had always seen herself as "Liz," spreads her wings as the "Blue Bird" destined for wider skies. And the "Liz" left standing in place, able only to gaze up as the other flies away, had been Nozomi all along. Every distant stare, every hesitant pause, and every unspoken trace of detachment from Nozomi earlier suddenly clicks seamlessly into place.

![Was this all part of your plan too, Reina Kousaka?](/post/essays/images/Pasted%20image%2020261121231419.png)

Yet even though those three minutes of musical eruption feel cathartic, they don't immediately solve what lies between the two of them. On the contrary, they shatter the delicate unspoken truce of the previous hour, bringing every long-buried conflict roaring to a head before handing the baton straight to the next scene. It isn't until the scene immediately following in the science classroom—with that clumsy yet candid embrace and confession—that all this heightened tension finally reaches resolution. Two people so used to walking on eggshells finally let their guards down, laying bare in the sunlight the truths they had hidden behind silence and forced cheerfulness. Afterward, the emotion ebbs back into everyday life like a receding tide. There is no forced melodrama, only a long, quiet exhale—from restraint and misalignment to release and resolution, the entire emotional flow comes cleanly full circle.

## Flawless Cinematography and Restrained Colors

Euphonium* TV series (well, it's actually because the character designer and animation director were different lol), the film's palette is far more restrained—finer linework, softer tones—yet its lighting, shadows, and depth-of-field blur remain exquisitely crafted. The whole movie feels airy and gentle, as if veiled in a faint wash of sunlight. The camera constantly moves across hallways, staircases, and windowsills, or lingers on fingers, footsteps, strands of hair, and fleeting micro-expressions. (~~So many leg shots.~~)
